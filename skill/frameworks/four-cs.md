# The Four Cs

The load bearing framework. Read it before writing any concept and before assigning any number out of 10.

Composition, Color, Clean Assets, Curiosity. A thumbnail that fails one of them fails, no matter how well it does the other three. That is the whole point of scoring against four axes instead of squinting at a picture and saying it looks good.

---

## Composition

Where things sit, and how big they are.

**Rule of thirds.** Subject on a third line, not dead center. The eye enters the frame at a third and travels across it. Centered subjects give the eye nowhere to go.

**The bottom right is not yours.** YouTube stamps the duration over the bottom right corner of every single thumbnail. Anything you put there, a face, a hand, a word, gets a black pill dropped on top of it. Keep it empty. This is the single most common unforced error in amateur thumbnails.

**Scale.** The test is never whether it looks good at full width. The test is whether the subject still reads at roughly 120 pixels wide, which is where most impressions actually happen. Design at that size, check at full size, not the other way round.

**Leading lines.** Diagonals that converge on the focal point: architecture, a corridor, a road, a row of anything, a gaze direction, a pointing gesture. Lines that lead nowhere are clutter wearing a nice hat.

**KISS.** One subject, one object, one context. Maximum. Three competing focal points means the eye picks none of them and scrolls past.

| Good | Bad |
| --- | --- |
| Subject on the left third, the payoff filling the right two thirds, bottom right empty, reads at any size | Subject centered, four props arranged around them, text in the bottom right where the timestamp lands |

## Color

**Contrast by opposition.** Pull subject and background from opposite sides of the color wheel. Red against green, yellow against purple, blue against orange. Complementary contrast is what makes a subject sit on top of a background instead of sinking into it.

Three pairings worth reaching for when the wheel is not giving enough separation:

- black and white together
- a highly saturated color on black
- white on a low saturation color

**Color makes a promise before a word is read.** Red reads danger and urgency. Blue reads intelligence and calm. Two thumbnails at identical craft level, one red and one blue, are selling two different videos. Pick the one that matches the video you actually made.

**Lighting.** Flat and bright beats moody, every time, at thumbnail size. A badly lit asset cannot be rescued in post; the fix always looks worse than shooting it correctly. Light the asset, do not repair it.

| Good | Bad |
| --- | --- |
| Warm lit subject against a deep cool background, separates instantly | Blue jacket against blue sky. Pretty at full size, invisible at 120 pixels |

---

## Clean Assets

Great assets beat clever edits. This is where most thumbnails are actually won or lost, and it is the least glamorous of the four.

**Composite, do not shoot in place.** The strongest frames are cut-out subjects placed onto a chosen background. That is what gives you control over scale, position, and contrast, which is to say control over composition itself.

**Where assets come from,** in order of preference:

1. Shoot it yourself, flat and bright. A modern phone in good light is genuinely enough.
2. Stock and image search, filtered hard by resolution.
3. AI generation, when the real thing cannot be obtained or photographed.

**Quality gate.** Soft cutout edges, a low resolution source, or baked-in shadows falling the wrong way will read as cheap at full size and as mud at 120 pixels. Never pull a still off your own video timeline and call it an asset; motion blur and compression make it obvious.

| Good | Bad |
| --- | --- |
| Subject shot on a plain wall in flat light, cut out cleanly, dropped on a high contrast ground | Subject cut from a dim phone photo, edge halo, then "fixed" with the brightness slider |

## Curiosity

The click is not paid for by beauty. It is paid for by an unanswered question. See `frameworks/curiosity-gap.md` for the full treatment.

| Lever | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Question | Sets up a question the video answers |
| Shock Factor | Shows something unexpected that the video reveals |
| Cliffhanger | Teases an outcome the video resolves |
| Unknown Knowledge | Shows information the video explains |

**Exaggerate interest, never reality.** The reason to stay honest here is mechanical, not moral: a thumbnail that oversells wins the click and loses the watch time, and watch time is what decides whether the next impression ever happens.

**Title and thumbnail are one unit.** The title sets the premise; the thumbnail adds information the title does not carry. When thumbnail text repeats the title, you had two slots and used one.

---

## Scoring

Score each C out of 10, then take the lowest, not the average. A frame that scores 10 on composition and 4 on curiosity is a 4, because nobody clicks it.

The pass bar is **8**. Below 8, name the failing C and give exactly one specific fix. Never write "make it punchier."

---

## Anti-Patterns

| Pattern | Why it fails |
| --- | --- |
| Subject dead center | Wastes the thirds structure, flattens the frame |
| Anything in the bottom right | YouTube's timestamp lands on it |
| Three focal points | The eye picks none and scrolls |
| Thumbnail text repeating the title | Two slots, one piece of information |
| Moody or backlit assets | Reads as mud at 120 pixels |
| Overselling the payoff | Wins the click, loses retention, costs the next impression |
| Designing before the idea is fixed | Good design plus a bad idea is still a bad thumbnail |
